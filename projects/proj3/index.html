Project 3: Flow Matching

CS 180, Fall 2026

In this project, we explore how to sample from and train flow matching models!

Part A: Exploring the Power of Flow Models

1. Visualizing Training and Sampling

1.1 Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

For this subpart, I will be using a photo of my dog Leo asleep on the couch.

1.2 Classical Denoising

Here we explore using Gaussian blurring as a means of denoising an image. As expected, we find that blurring the image smooths it out while losing some of the details. It's not bad, but we can do much better!

1.3 One-Step Denoising

We now use a pretrained model to denoise our image in one step, starting from various noise levels.

1.4 Visualizing Velocity

Here, we visualize the errors in our model's predictions across various sigma levels. We find that our models' errors are greater in magntitude at higher noise levels, which intuitively makes sense.

1.5 Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

Now, instead of using a one-shot denoised prediction from our model, we implement Euler sampling to iteratively denoise an image bit by bit. With Euler sampling, we make several denoiser calls for a single image, and take small steps in the right direction each time.

We naturally find that Euler sampling produces the most accurate denoised image, followed by a one-step denoiser and lastly Gaussian blurring.

1.6 Generating Images from Noise

Here, we prompt our model to generate a "a high quality photo". It's quite interesting that despite changing the seed, our model consistently produces human-looking images, which suggests that most images in its training set captioned as "high quality" contained humans. It was also interesting how the model was unable to produce the humans correctly, like it had a fuzzy understanding of what a high quality image should look like, but couldn't quite fill in the details.

2. Conditional Generation and Guidance

2.1 Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

We now use the same prompt and seeds as above, but use classifier-free guidance to increase adherence to the prompt. Now, we find that the model is able to fill in the human details correctly and the photos look much more realistic than in the previous section. My explanation for this would be that CFG pushes the model to commit to a specific mode in its output distribution, whereas sampling without guidance produces more of an "average" image without heavily committing to any direction. On the other hand, we do find we have sacrificed some level of diversity as our sampled images are now much more similar in nature.

3. Creative Applications

3.1 Image-to-Image Editing

In this section we use the same prompt of "a high quality photo" but start from noised versions of three base images: the Campanile, my dog Leo, and an image of Cristiano Ronaldo hitting the classic "siuuu" celebration.

3.2 Text-Guided Image Editing

We now change the prompt to see if we can try edit our images. I particularly liked how sunglasses Ronaldo edit turned out.

3.3 Visual Anagrams

We now produce visual anagrams by denoising an upright image in one prompt direction, denoising its flipped image in another direction, and taking the average velocity to produce an anagram image! I really like how these turned out.

3.4 Hybrid Images

Similar to the previous project we create hybrid images, this time by combining high-pass and low-pass filters with denoising. The first image is of a mountain range and an old man's face, and the second is of an ocean wave and a dragon's head.

Part B: Flow Matching from Scratch!

In this section we train our own flow matching model on the MNIST handwritten digit dataset!

Part 1: Training a Single-Step Denoising UNet

1.2 Using the UNet to Train a Denoiser

Let's first take a look at our training data at various noise levels.

1.2.1 Training

Our first task is to train a model to denoise images with a fixed σ of 0.5. We use the UNet architecture with a hidden dimensions of 128, and visualize our training loss below.

We trained our model for five epochs, and now compare our Epoch 1 checkpoint with our Epoch 5 checkpoint. There's little difference, and this is corroborated by our loss curve, our model's learning is mostly complete by the end of the first epoch.

1.2.2 Out-of-Distribution Testing

We now visualize our model's performance on inputs at varying sigma levels. Even though our model was only trained on images noised to σ=0.5, it performs pretty well on all inputs below σ = 0.6, after which it starts to struggle.

1.2.3 Denoising Pure Noise

Our next experiment is to train our model to generate images starting from pure noise. We find that under this current formulation, our model's diversity collapses and it's unable to generate real digits. Rather, it defaults to predicting an image that looks like the average of all the numbers. This makes sense under it's current objective, because MSE loss would be minimized when predicting the average of all possible images from random noise.

Part 2: Training a Flow Matching Model

2.2 Training the Time-Conditioned UNet

We attempt to fix the issue from the previous part by conditioning our model on a given timestep/sigma level.

2.3 Sampling from the Time-Conditioned UNet

We find that our time-conditioned UNet is able to generate more realistic outputs than the unconditioned pure-noise network, but there's still room for improvement.

2.5 Training the Class-Conditioned UNet

We now train a UNet that is conditioned both on the noise level and on a target digit.

2.6 Sampling from the Class-Conditioned UNet

We see that this class-conditioned UNet converges quite quickly as seen in the loss curve above. We also compare using an exponential learning rate scheduler with a constant learning rate. Since the exponential LR scheduler starts at 1e-2 and then decays, I chose the fixed learning rate model to use a smaller LR of 1e-3 to compensate for the fact that we're not decaying. Comparing the epoch 1 outputs of both models we find the fixed LR model actually converges faster, although they end up converging to a similar performance level in the end.

Takeaways

My biggest takeaway from this project was developing an intuition for flow matching, where we break down the one-shot image generation problem into a series of more tractable subproblems, gradually denoising an image at each step. I particularly enjoyed the visual anagrams formulation because the math behind it is so intuitive. We essentially split the task into two separate denoising problems and take the average predicted direction. It's amazing how, even though the diffusion model wasn't explicitly trained for such a task, we can leverage our understanding of what it's predicting to generate entirely new kinds of images.
